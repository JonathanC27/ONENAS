\section{Dataset}
The Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) dataset is the standard academic source for U.S. equity prices, returns, and trading volumes, and it is widely used for empirical asset pricing and financial forecasting research. The datasets used in this paper are derived from the CRSP dataset, using daily frequency data.

\subsection{Portfolios}
\label{subsec:validation-portfolios}

We choose mid-cap companies drawn from the S\&P 500, chosen because mid-cap stocks receive less analyst and institutional scrutiny than large-caps and so are less efficiently priced. We then shuffled the stocks and made $V1$--$V4$, of 50 stocks each.
The target universe $\mathcal{U}$ covers mid-cap through low-large-cap securities, defined by current market capitalization $2 \le M_i < 50$ in USD billions for security~$i$.
Restricting the S\&P~500 CRSP panel to securities with at least 20 years of continuous trading history leaves 203 names in this range, from which 200 were drawn at random to form $\mathcal{U}$.
These 200 stocks were then shuffled and split evenly into $V1$--$V4$, so the four portfolios are disjoint and no stock appears in more than one.

Each portfolio is stored as one series per ticker together with a
panel-level date index carrying the aligned raw price and
transaction-cost columns for every name. All four end on 2024-12-31 but
begin on different dates, since a portfolio starts only once all 50 of
its constituents have priced history: $V1$ from 2004-08-16, $V2$ from
2004-12-17, $V3$ from 2004-10-27 and $V4$ from 2004-02-09, giving
5{,}130, 5{,}043, 5{,}079 and 5{,}257 trading days respectively. The
differing lengths affect only how much burn-in each portfolio receives;
the tuning and trading periods are the same calendar spans for all four,
so no method gains or loses scored days.

\subsection{Features and Prediction Target}

Each model sees seven per-stock features. Three describe the stock's own
recent price behaviour, three describe how easily and how heavily it
trades, and one places it against the rest of the market that day. All
are computed causally, from information available at or before the day
on which a position is formed.

Liquidity and attention features are included because the target is a
short-horizon one. Over a single day, how expensive a stock is to trade
and how much attention it is currently attracting carry more information
about its next move than fundamentals do, and they also tell the model
where a predicted edge would be eaten by trading costs.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \texttt{RET} --- the raw daily return.
\item \texttt{RET\_CS\_IN} --- the same return expressed as a
      cross-sectional rank-normal score,
      $\Phi^{-1}\!\big((\mathrm{rank}_t(i)-0.5)/N\big)$ over the $N$
      names priced that day. It tells the model how a stock did
      \emph{relative to its peers}, which is what the target asks about.
\item \texttt{BA\_SPREAD} --- the quoted bid--ask spread, the gap between
      the best buying and selling price, which proxies how costly the
      stock is to trade.
\item \texttt{ILLIQUIDITY} --- Amihud illiquidity: absolute return over
      dollar volume, smoothed over 21 days and logged. It is large when
      a small amount of trading moves the price a lot.
\item \texttt{REV21\_1} --- short-horizon reversal: the compounded return
      over the 20 days before the formation day, excluding it. Recent
      winners tend to give some of it back, so this is informative with
      a negative sign.
\item \texttt{TURN\_RATIO} --- turnover acceleration: recent trading
      activity relative to its own longer-run level, as the mean of
      $\log(1+\mathrm{turnover})$ over the last 5 days minus the same
      mean over the last 63. It rises when a stock begins attracting
      unusual attention.
\item \texttt{VOL21} --- realised volatility, the standard deviation of
      \texttt{RET} over the trailing 21 days.
\end{itemize}

These are standard predictors in the empirical asset pricing literature
rather than novel ones; the contribution of this paper is the
architecture search, so the feature set is deliberately conventional.

The last five are additionally mapped, per date, to their
cross-sectional rank on $[-1,1]$. Without this a feature would carry a
level that drifts across two decades --- spreads and volumes in 2004 are
not comparable to 2024 --- and the model would spend capacity tracking
that drift instead of the cross-section.

The prediction target is \texttt{RET\_CS}: the next day's return, rank-
normalised across the cross-section of that day by the same $\Phi^{-1}$
transform. Predicting a cross-sectional rank rather than a raw return
makes the target stationary by construction and matches how the
predictions are consumed --- every trading rule in this paper reads only
the ordering of the predicted cross-section.

Realised returns used for scoring are CRSP \texttt{RET}, which is adjusted
for splits and dividends. Prices and per-name transaction costs enter only
through the cost model below.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Online Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

ONE-NAS operates on a weekly clock. Each panel's daily series is divided
into consecutive windows of 5 trading days, and one generation is
executed per window. At generation $t$, the revealed data are split into
two parts: the 5 most recent windows form the validation block, on which
all genomes are scored to select the elite sets, and every earlier
window forms the training pool, from which offspring sample subsequences
of $L=40$ days. The genomes selected at generation $t$ then predict the
next, unrevealed window day by day as its features arrive. When that
window closes, its realised returns are appended to the revealed data,
the validation block advances by one window, and generation $t{+}1$
begins. The training pool thus grows by one window per generation while
the validation block and the predicted window slide forward with it.

Leakage is prevented by construction. A window is predicted before any
of its labels are revealed, enters the validation block only after it
has been predicted, and enters the training pool only after it has left
the validation block. No genome is ever trained or selected on data it
is later scored on, and no model is refit on the scored span with
hindsight. Baselines are evaluated under the same prequential rule:
predict the next day, reveal the outcome, update.

The sample is divided into three disjoint calendar spans. The
\textbf{burn-in} span (from each portfolio's first date through
2015-12-31) is used only to grow the population from the minimal seed
genome; no prediction is recorded. The \textbf{tuning} span
(2016--2019) is where every configuration decision is made. The
\textbf{trading} span (2022--2024) is what all reported results are
measured on, and is also the period evaluated by the prior study on
these panels, so the two are directly comparable. Evolution is
uninterrupted across all three: the population continues to evolve
online through the tuning span and into the trading span without any
configuration change. In every case 50 warm-up generations precede the
first recorded prediction, so the population that begins trading is
mature rather than cold-started.

The search is single-axis around the control configuration: each cell

(panel, seed) cells. Sixteen configurations were trained --- the control
plus fifteen variants:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Fitness signal.} Selection on validation MSE (control),
      on cross-sectional rank IC, and on a gated rank IC.
\item \textbf{Prediction target.} 1-day cross-sectional rank-normal
      return (control) and the 5-day equivalent, the latter also in
      combination with gated-IC selection.
\item \textbf{Population structure.} Elite/generated ratios of 8/5
      (control), 5/10 and 5/15; repopulation every 25, 50 (control) and
      100 generations.
\item \textbf{Training budget.} 2000 (control) and 600 training
      subsequences per genome; 10 (control) and 20 backpropagation
      epochs per genome; one (control) and two generations per window.
\item \textbf{Replay sampling.} Prioritised replay at
      $\alpha \in \{0.4, 0.6\ (\text{control}), 0.8\}$, at
      $\lambda \in \{0.007\ (\text{control}), 0.021\}$, and uniform

\item \textbf{Island count.} 8, 16, 20, 40, 50 and 60
      (Table~\ref{tab:tunewidth}).
\end{itemize}

Selection used the tuning-span rank IC as the primary objective with the
registered book's net return and Sharpe as an economic co-primary, and
required a paired improvement over the control to adopt a change. No
single-axis variant met that bar, so the control configuration is the one
reported; the island count is the one axis where a change was adopted,
and the value chosen sits on the plateau of Table~\ref{tab:tunewidth}
rather than at its nominal maximum.

Several axes were deliberately not searched, having been settled by
earlier work on this data: sequence length, the number of validation
sets, the node-type library, window geometry, and the ensemble's width
and combination rule.

\subsection{Baselines}

ONE-NAS is compared against four online baselines. All are evaluated on
the same panels, with the same prequential protocol, target, trading
books, and transaction costs. Each baseline is a single model, and
reported figures are means over independent runs rather than the return
of an ensemble of those runs.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Online LSTM.} A single-layer LSTM with 8 hidden units and
      input sequence length 10, trained with Adam (learning rate
      $10^{-3}$). The model takes one gradient step per trading day on a
      minibatch drawn from a replay buffer of the most recent 1000 days.
\item \textbf{Online GRU.} Identical to the online LSTM with a GRU cell
      and sequence length 5.
\item \textbf{Periodic LSTM.} The same LSTM architecture, retrained from
      scratch at the start of each month for up to 4000 steps and held
      fixed between retrainings. This represents the batch-retraining
      practice common in production forecasting systems.
\item \textbf{Online AR.} A first-order autoregressive model fitted by
      online gradient descent with learning rate $10^{-4}$. The model is
      deterministic and contributes one run per panel.
\end{itemize}

The recurrent baselines represent fixed-architecture online learning at
the same model scale as the networks ONE-NAS evolves; the difference
between them and ONE-NAS is therefore whether the architecture is
searched and whether a population is maintained, not model capacity.

\subsection{Trading Books}

A \emph{book} is the rule that turns a day's predicted ranking into
positions, and it is what converts prediction quality into money. All
three books used here are \emph{long--short}: they buy (go \emph{long})
the names predicted to do best and sell borrowed shares of (go
\emph{short}) the names predicted to do worst, in equal dollar amounts.
Equal amounts on each side leave the book roughly
\emph{dollar-neutral}, so it profits from the top names outperforming
the bottom ones rather than from the market rising. Its returns are
therefore not comparable to simply holding the market.

Predictions are converted into positions by a pre-registered primary
book, with two further books reported as sensitivities. Every book reads
only the ordering of each day's predicted cross-section, so all reported
metrics are invariant to any strictly monotone transform of a model's
output.

\paragraph{Overlapping portfolios.} The primary book follows
the overlapping-portfolio construction of \citet{efficiency1993returns}.
Each day a new portfolio is formed that goes long the $k=10$ highest-ranked
names and short the 10 lowest-ranked, with equal notional per name, and
is held for $H=10$ trading days; the portfolio formed $H$ days earlier is
closed. Ten portfolios are therefore open at any time, each funded with
$1/H$ of the capital, so the book replaces about a tenth of itself per
day rather than rebuilding daily. Positions are netted across portfolios
before trading, so a name that appears in several consecutive portfolios
is held rather than repeatedly bought and sold, and transaction costs are
charged only on the change in aggregate notional per name.

\paragraph{Banded entry and exit.} The second book uses the buy/hold
spread of \citet{novy2016taxonomy}, a standard device for reducing
turnover: a name is entered when its predicted rank reaches the top 10
and is held until its rank falls past 35. Names between the two
thresholds are neither bought nor sold, which removes the churn caused
by small day-to-day changes in rank near the boundary.

\paragraph{Conditional long--short.} The third book is the daily
long--short rule of the prior study on these panels. Each day, if the
predicted return of the $k$-th ranked name is positive and that of the
$k$-th lowest-ranked name is negative, all existing positions are
closed and the capital is redeployed equally across the top $k$ names
long and the bottom $k$ names short; otherwise the current positions are
held unchanged. The condition is a confidence gate that suspends
rebalancing when the model does not separate the top and bottom of the
cross-section by sign. On rank-normalised predictions the gate is
satisfied every day, so this book reduces to daily full rebalancing.

\paragraph{Capital and costs.} Each book is funded with \$100 of capital
and, when fully invested, holds \$100 long and \$100 short, so \$200 of
stock is held in total --- the \emph{gross notional}.

Trading is not free, and the cost is charged explicitly rather than
assumed away. Each stock has a per-share cost $c_{i,t}$ in dollars,
taken from the dataset's \texttt{TRAN\_COST} field, which is the half
bid--ask spread: the cost of crossing the spread once, in one direction.
Dividing by the share price converts it to a cost per dollar traded, and
this is applied to the notional actually traded at the previous close. A
trade of $\$v$ in stock~$i$ therefore pays
\begin{equation*}
  \text{cost} \;=\; v \cdot \frac{c_{i,t}}{|P_{i,t}|},
\end{equation*}
which is a few basis points for the median name in these portfolios and
considerably more for the least liquid ones. Costs are per-stock and
time-varying rather than a single flat basis-point assumption, so
illiquid names are penalised as they should be. All returns reported in
this paper are \emph{net}: these costs have already been deducted.

\subsection{Metrics}

Two families of metrics are reported. \emph{Prediction quality} is
measured by the rank information coefficient (rank IC): the Spearman
correlation between predicted and realised returns across the names on
a given day, averaged over all scored days. Because the trading books
read only the ordering of predictions, rank IC is the accuracy measure
that the books directly depend on. Values are small by construction ---
a single day's cross-sectional return is mostly noise, and a rank IC of
$0.01$--$0.02$ is a usable signal in this setting rather than a weak
one.

\emph{Economic performance} is measured on each trading book by four
quantities: net return, the cumulative daily profit and loss after
transaction costs as a fraction of initial capital; annualised Sharpe
ratio, the mean daily return divided by its standard deviation and
scaled by $\sqrt{252}$, which measures return per unit of risk; maximum
drawdown, the largest peak-to-trough decline in cumulative return over
the period, that is the worst loss an investor entering at the worst
moment would have sat through; and turnover, the mean daily traded
notional as a fraction of gross book size, so that a turnover of
$0.12$ means about $12\%$ of the book is replaced each day.

To test whether returns are explained by exposure to known sources of
market risk, each arm's pooled daily book return is regressed on the
Fama--French three factors \cite{fama1993common} and the momentum
factor \cite{carhart1997persistence}. The intercept of this regression
(alpha) is the return not attributable to those factors, and is
therefore the evidence of skill rather than of risk-taking; the market
coefficient (beta) is the sensitivity to overall market movement, near
$1$ for a portfolio that moves with the market and near $0$ for one
insulated from it. Standard errors are Newey--West
\cite{newey1986simple} with lag 10 to account for autocorrelation
induced by the 10-day holding period.

\subsection{Replication and Statistical Comparison}

The unit of replication is one (panel, seed) cell. Unless stated
otherwise, each ONE-NAS configuration is run with 10 seeds on 4 panels,
giving 40 cells. Baselines were run at higher replication (48 seeds for
online LSTM, 40 for online GRU and periodic LSTM) and their means are
reported at that replication.

Comparisons between arms are paired within (panel, seed) cells so that
panel and seed effects cancel, and reported $t$ statistics are paired
$t$-tests over those cells. When two arms do not share a seed set, the
pairing is restricted to their common cells and $n$ is stated.

\subsection{Compute Environment}

All searches ran on the ACCESS Anvil cluster. Each run occupied one
node of the \texttt{wholenode} partition with 128 MPI ranks: one master
maintaining the island populations and generating offspring, and 127
workers training and evaluating them. The island count is a search
parameter independent of the rank count, so the worker pool is
identical across all reported configurations. Baselines are
single-process and were run on the same hardware. The inference
endpoint described in Section~\ref{sec:deployment} is a Raspberry Pi 4B with 8GB RAM.

\subsection{Pre-Registration}

The scored configuration, trading period, trading book, and metrics
were committed to the repository before the trading period was scored.
Every subsequent analysis of trading-period data was declared in
advance with its decision rule, and any configuration change required a
dated amendment written before the changed configuration was scored.
The registered protocol, the full hyperparameter screen with per-cell
outcomes, and all declared exploratory analyses are included in the
code release.
